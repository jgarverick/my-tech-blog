## Why talk about the psyche at all?

Anyone who has spent the last twelve to eighteen months in this industry has felt the ground move. Between AI-assisted coding, agentic systems, and a steady stream of headlines about what engineers will or won't be doing in a few years, there's a very real undercurrent of unease in our community. I've had more than a few conversations with colleagues, clients, and friends who are quietly wondering where they fit once the dust settles.

So why spend a conference slot on that, rather than on a new framework or a deployment pattern? Because the way we think about our own roles has a direct impact on how well we adapt, and it's important to note that this is not the first time we've been here. Taking the time to name that unease, rather than letting it sit in the background, is the first step toward doing something productive with it.

## We've been here before

The first part of the session looked back at earlier shifts and how engineers came through them. I started writing BASIC in a fifth-grade computer lab, moved through VB and into .NET when it was still new, and then spent a good stretch of my career on ALM, DevOps, containers, and the move to the cloud. Each of those transitions came with its own version of "this changes everything," and each one did change a great deal, just not in the way the loudest voices predicted.

Of course, the current shift is moving faster and touching more of the job at once than most of the earlier ones did. Granted, speed alone doesn't make it unprecedented. The pattern I keep seeing is the same one from past transitions: the people who come out ahead are the ones who reframe what they bring to the table, rather than holding tightly to the specific tasks they used to perform.

## Exercise one: map your week

The first exercise is simple, and I'd encourage you to try it right now. Look back at the past week of your work and write down what you actually spent time on, then map each item to a common functional area for a technologist. Some items will land in hands-on execution, some in coordinating work across people and systems, and some in translating between the business and the technical teams.

What most people find is that a surprising share of their week already lives outside of pure execution, whether that's reviewing designs, clarifying requirements, or explaining tradeoffs to a stakeholder. That's the work that tends to become more valuable as tools take on more of the execution, and it's worth seeing on paper how much of it you're already doing.

## Exercise two: rewrite your bio

The second exercise asks you to rewrite your professional bio, or your personal brand statement, to reflect how your thinking has changed. Resist the urge to simply swap in new buzzwords. Instead, look at the results of the first exercise and ask whether your bio describes the work you actually do and want to keep doing, or the work you did five years ago.

To make that concrete, I put up a slide I called "Same Person, Different Story," which compared how we often describe ourselves with how we could describe ourselves. An architect might say, "I design systems and create architecture diagrams." That's accurate, but it ties your identity to the artifact, so when AI generates diagrams faster than you can, it's easy to feel threatened. The same architect could instead say, "I make judgment calls about complex systems under organizational constraints." That version ties your identity to the judgment, and AI generating diagrams faster now leaves you feeling amplified rather than replaced.

| How we often describe ourselves | How we could describe ourselves |
| --- | --- |
| "I design systems and create architecture diagrams." | "I make judgment calls about complex systems under organizational constraints." |
| When AI generates diagrams faster, we feel threatened. | When AI generates diagrams faster, we feel amplified. |
| Our identity is tied to the artifact. | Our identity is tied to the judgment. |

The takeaway from that slide is the line along the bottom: same skills, same experience, different story. Nothing about the person changes between the two columns; only the framing does, and that framing has a lot to do with whether the current shift feels like a threat or an opportunity.

## Wrapping up

The engineers I talk with aren't short on skill; what many of them are missing is a clear picture of how those skills translate into what comes next. Taking an honest look at how you spend your time, and then describing yourself in those terms, goes a long way toward turning an uneasy moment into an opportunity.
